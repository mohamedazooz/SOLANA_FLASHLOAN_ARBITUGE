# 42 wallets, 73 tokens, one repeated PumpSwap lifecycle

- **Date**: 2026-08-30
- **Original URL**: [https://onchaindivers.substack.com/p/42-wallets-73-tokens-one-repeated](https://onchaindivers.substack.com/p/42-wallets-73-tokens-one-repeated)
- **Description**: We found a dense co-trading cluster through our Solana indexer.

---

<p>We found a dense co-trading cluster through our Solana indexer. Its launches appeared to produce a 20x first-minute move. Correct transaction alignment erased that signal, exposed a repeatable launcher-and-bot sequence, and showed why an 86% win rate can still lose money.</p><p><strong>Research note</strong>: The analysis uses a delayed, fixed historical window. Wallet and mint addresses are intentionally withheld. The chart had to be rebuilt.</p><h4><strong>How we found the cluster </strong></h4><p>We used a fixed 72-hour UTC window:</p><p><strong>[2026-08-25 14:09:00, 2026-08-28 14:09:00]</strong></p><p>The token universe contained <strong>4,516 Pump.fun tokens</strong> that migrated to PumpSwap during the same window. We joined trades only to each token’s first migration pool and ignored pre-migration activity.</p><p>The broad universe contained <strong>1,087,529</strong> signing wallets. We then applied a deliberately strict activity <strong>rule</strong>:</p><p>— the wallet traded no more than 2% of the migrated token universe, or at most 90 tokens;<br>— on every token it touched, the wallet made at least four buys and four sells;<br>— trade counts were deduplicated by signature.</p><p>That left <strong>41,156</strong> wallets.</p><p>We reduced the dataset to a binary wallet-token incidence matrix:</p><div class="highlighted_code_block" data-attrs="{&quot;language&quot;:&quot;plaintext&quot;,&quot;nodeId&quot;:&quot;382d7795-3a68-4ff1-9582-2dffd83c506f&quot;}" data-component-name="HighlightedCodeBlockToDOM"><pre class="shiki"><code class="language-plaintext">X[token, wallet] = 1 if the wallet traded the token</code></pre></div><p>The first co-occurrence result was mostly noise. A few severe pool collapses dominated the average.</p><p>We then split the 73 tokens chronologically by anchor slot and tx_idx. Strategy parameters were selected on the first 36 tokens and applied unchanged to the final 37.</p><p>The selected short-horizon rule used a small take-profit, a nominal 5% stop, and a 30-second timeout. On the final 37 tokens it produced:</p><p>— 32 take-profit exits and five stop exits;<br>— an 86.5% win rate;<br>— a median return of +2.13%;<br>— a total loss of 0.147 SOL;<br>— a mean loss of 0.00398 SOL per trade;<br>— a worst return of -87.75%.</p><p>That combination is easy to misread. The strategy won often. It still lost money.</p><h4><strong>Why the stop did not stop the loss ?</strong></h4><p>A nominal stop assumes that the market offers an executable state near the threshold. These pools often did not.</p><p>The strategy observed a safe state, then the next relevant transaction moved the pool far beyond the stop. By the time a reactive sell could land, the executable loss was sometimes 30%, 40%, or more than 80%.</p><p>This is not ordinary slippage around a continuous order book. It is gap risk in an AMM with concentrated, rapidly removable quote liquidity.</p><p>We tried a causal filter using only information visible immediately after the large sell:</p><p>— the sell’s price impact;<br>— post-sell price relative to the anchor;<br>— effective quote liquidity.</p><p>The filter improved the early half. It failed on the later half. The late sample still had an 86.4% win rate, a positive median, negative mean PnL, and the same 87.75% worst case.</p><p>Smaller size reduced ordinary impact. It did not reduce the percentage loss caused by a later pool collapse.</p><h4><strong>What the cluster tells us ?</strong></h4><p>The cluster is useful, but not for the reason suggested by the first chart.</p><p>It shows that wallet-token topology can reveal recurring launch infrastructure before any entity labels exist. Forty-two wallets repeatedly chose the same 73 tokens, traded both sides on every token, and supplied most of the re-buy flow after a launcher-linked sell.</p><p>It also shows why transaction alignment matters. Migration-time normalization accidentally credited a reactive strategy with a price move created by a transaction that had already completed. Re-anchoring at the post-transaction reserve state removed the apparent 20x opportunity.</p><p>Finally, the cluster is a clean example of the difference between prediction and execution. The second buy wave was predictable in aggregate. Prediction accuracy was not enough. The loss distribution depended on whether the pool remained sellable after entry.</p><h4><strong>What we are not claiming</strong></h4><ul><li><p>We are <strong>NOT</strong> claiming that the 42 wallets share one owner. The evidence establishes repeated co-trading and near-identical token selection.</p></li><li><p>We are not claiming that the launcher and cohort coordinate off-chain. The transaction sequence is consistent with coordinated infrastructure, but other explanations remain possible.</p></li><li><p>We are not publishing wallet addresses, token addresses, or a live trigger. The historical strategy had negative expected PnL after fees even before tips and failed landings.</p></li><li><p>We are also not treating second-level timestamps as event order. Every causal boundary in this analysis uses slot and tx_idx, followed by the complete transaction boundary and reserve continuity where needed.</p><p></p></li></ul><h4><strong>Reproducing the framework</strong></h4><p>The discovery stage requires a binary wallet-token incidence table rather than a dense wallet-by-wallet matrix:</p><div class="highlighted_code_block" data-attrs="{&quot;language&quot;:&quot;sql&quot;,&quot;nodeId&quot;:&quot;559ec4f7-f983-4bbc-91e6-595cafde4d90&quot;}" data-component-name="HighlightedCodeBlockToDOM"><pre class="shiki"><code class="language-sql">SELECT
signing_wallet AS wallet,
base_token AS mint,
direction,
uniqExact(signature) AS transactions
FROM pumpswap_all_swaps
WHERE block_time &gt;= toDateTime(’2026-08-25 14:09:00’, ‘UTC’)
AND block_time &lt; toDateTime(’2026-08-28 14:09:00’, ‘UTC’)
GROUP BY wallet, mint, direction</code></pre></div><p>In production, the query also joins each mint to its exact migration pool, rejects pre-migration swaps, and runs in restartable time batches.</p><p>The causal tape should always be ordered as:</p><div class="highlighted_code_block" data-attrs="{&quot;language&quot;:&quot;plaintext&quot;,&quot;nodeId&quot;:&quot;2f5431eb-8e18-4750-bc55-db36dc63c434&quot;}" data-component-name="HighlightedCodeBlockToDOM"><pre class="shiki"><code class="language-plaintext">transactions = transactions.sort_values([”mint”, “slot”, “tx_idx”, “signature”])</code></pre></div><p>For a multi-instruction anchor, do not stop at the first matching row. Reconstruct the full signature and anchor the strategy at its final reserve state.</p><p>The public code sketch omits wallet lists, thresholds used for cluster selection, and landing configuration. Those are operational parameters rather than necessary ingredients for understanding the result.</p><p></p><h4><strong>Conclusion</strong></h4><p>We found a remarkably consistent PumpSwap co-trading cluster through our indexer: 42 wallets, 73 identical token choices, a repeatable launcher anchor, a large sell on every token, and a cohort-dominated re-buy wave.</p><p>The tempting trade was to buy immediately after the launcher. It failed. The more promising trade was to buy after the large sell. It won more than 86% of the time in the late sample and still lost money because a few pools became nearly unsellable.</p><p>That is the finding worth keeping. In launch markets, a recurring flow pattern can be real, highly predictable, and economically negative at the same time. The missing variable is not another entry threshold.
